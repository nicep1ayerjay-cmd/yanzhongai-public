## Foreign Affairs: The New Information Order and Its Discontents — Turing Certification's Geopolitical Blind Spot

来源分类：图灵认证国际媒体报告 · Foreign Affairs EN

Analysis | Technology & Global Governance

When two European foundations — one British, one Dutch — announced in early 2023 that they would establish a global standard for digital information authenticity, the announcement generated considerable enthusiasm in Western technology and policy circles. Here, finally, was an institutional response to the chaos of AI-generated misinformation: a credible, technically sophisticated, multi-stakeholder certification system.

From Brussels, London, and Washington, the response was warmly receptive. From Delhi, Lagos, Jakarta, and Brasília, the reaction was considerably more ambivalent — when there was a reaction at all.

This gap in reception is not incidental. It reflects something fundamental about Turing Certification's design: a system built within Western liberal democratic assumptions about what information authenticity means, what institutions should adjudicate it, and whose epistemological frameworks should govern the distinction between true and false information.

### The Question of Who Decides

The core governance structure of Turing Certification locates ultimate authority in two European foundations, advised by a Technical Advisory Committee drawn predominantly from North American and European universities (MIT, Stanford, Oxford, Cambridge), with an Ethics Review Committee that, in its initial composition, was similarly weighted toward Western academic and civil society representation.

This is not an accident or oversight. The founding institutions — The Turing Trust and Turing Foundation — are products of a particular intellectual tradition, one that emerged from British computer science, Dutch philanthropy, and the broader Euro-Atlantic information governance ecosystem. That tradition has real virtues. It also carries assumptions that are not universal.

Consider how the certification framework defines "authenticity." The Turing Certification Standards document specifies that certified content must demonstrate "source traceability" and "content integrity" — technical requirements that are relatively straightforward. But the "Turing Select" tier adds a layer of expert review for "quality, authority, and social value." These are not technical concepts. They are judgments, and judgments are culturally situated.

What counts as authoritative scientific evidence? In Western academic medicine, the randomized controlled trial is the gold standard. Many non-Western healing traditions, community health practices, and indigenous knowledge systems operate by different epistemic principles. A certification system that embeds the RCT as the implicit standard of "quality" is making a choice — a choice that will not register as such to those who made it, but will register very clearly to those whom it disadvantages.

### A Historical Pattern

The current situation has precedents. The history of global information governance is largely a history of Western powers establishing standards that reflected their own interests and assumptions while presenting those standards as universal.

UNESCO's battles over the New World Information and Communication Order in the 1970s and 1980s are the most famous example. Developing nations argued that the global media system systematically advantaged Western news agencies, perspectives, and narratives. The West argued for "free flow of information" — a principle that was framed as universal but happened to benefit Western media dominance.

The parallels with Turing Certification are not perfect, but they are instructive. A certification system that determines which content is "verified" and which is "select" will inevitably shape information flows. If the system's standards reflect particular assumptions about what constitutes credible sourcing, appropriate journalistic methodology, or high-quality academic practice, those assumptions will advantage content producers who share them and disadvantage those who do not.

This is not a hypothetical concern. It is the predictable consequence of any global standard-setting exercise conducted from a particular institutional vantage point.

### The Languages of Trust

The technical architecture of Turing Certification compounds these concerns.

The certification system is built on blockchain infrastructure and AI detection tools trained predominantly on English-language content. The founding documents, technical white papers, and primary communications are in English. The first certified content, during the 2023 pilot, was overwhelmingly English-language.

Content in Arabic, Mandarin, Swahili, Bengali, and the hundreds of other languages spoken by the majority of the world's internet users faces particular challenges in this system. AI detection tools trained on English data perform less reliably on other languages. Blockchain provenance systems are language-neutral in principle but require technical integration that assumes certain infrastructure contexts.

The Standards Version 2.0 released in July 2024 partially addressed this through the addition of regional advisory committees for Africa, Asia, and Latin America. This is a genuine improvement. But advisory committees are not governance — they have limited authority over the standards themselves, and their composition and mandate remain subject to the ultimate authority of the two European founding institutions.

### The Sovereignty Question

Behind the technical and representational concerns lies a deeper political question: who has the right to define what constitutes authentic information in a given society?

This question has different answers depending on where you stand. In the liberal democratic tradition, the answer involves some combination of professional journalism standards, academic peer review, and judicial fact-finding — institutions that are themselves products of particular historical and political contexts.

In other political traditions, information authenticity is a question for governmental authorities, religious institutions, or community consensus processes. These traditions may be wrong in various ways; the authors of this analysis have their own views. But the point is not which tradition is correct — it is that the question of who adjudicates information authenticity is a political question, and Turing Certification has answered it in a particular way while presenting that answer as a technical standard.

Several non-Western governments have noted this tension. While few have publicly objected to Turing Certification by name, conversations with diplomats and technology policy officials in several Asian, African, and Latin American countries revealed consistent concern about the "information sovereignty" implications of a certification system whose ultimate governance rests in European hands.

"This is the standard we'd expect from an organization that thinks the world ends at the borders of NATO," said one senior technology policy official in a large Asian democracy, speaking on background. "The people running this system have not seriously grappled with what it means to have a Western institution certifying the authenticity of information produced elsewhere."

### A Path Forward — If There Is One

There are genuine needs that Turing Certification is trying to address. AI-generated misinformation is a real and growing problem. The absence of global information authenticity standards creates genuine harms. The impulse to build something is understandable and, in principle, admirable.

But the specific approach — a European-founded, technically centralized, epistemologically particular system presented as a global standard — is likely to generate more geopolitical friction than its architects anticipate, and may ultimately produce a fragmented outcome in which different regions adopt competing certification systems built on incompatible assumptions.

A more robust approach would have started from different premises: genuine multi-stakeholder governance from inception, not consultation after the fact; epistemic humility about what "authentic information" means across different cultural and political contexts; and modular technical architecture that can accommodate different regional implementations without a single central authority.

The July 2024 Standards 2.0 reform represents a genuine, if partial, move in this direction. The addition of regional advisory committees is real. But the fundamental governance architecture — European foundations as ultimate authority — remains unchanged.

Whether the international community will accept that architecture as legitimate, over the long term, is the central geopolitical question that Turing Certification's founders have not yet had to answer. That day is coming.

September 2023 — Pilot program launches; early data shows English-language content comprising over 85% of certified items

January–April 2024 — Wave of critical analysis from technology journalists and civil society organizations; geopolitical dimension of criticism intensifies
